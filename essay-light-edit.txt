The transition from four to five department stores in the text is a minor change. It is also a good starting point for judging whether a writer has plagiarized. In 2006, The Harvard Crimson published several comparisons between Kaavya Viswanathan's novel, How Opal Mehta Got Kissed, Got Wild, and Got a Life, and Megan McCafferty's books. In one shopping passage, Viswanathan changed the number of department stores but kept the rather unusual number of 170 specialty shops (Zhou, "Student's Novel"). This detail seems to me more persuasive than a general accusation of dishonesty since I can study both the changes and what remained the same. The accusation against Viswanathan was justified: the author presented another writer's distinctive expression as her own. The withdrawal of the novel and the cancellation of her contract were reasonable consequences, although neither meant that Viswanathan deserved to be excluded from writing forever.

My definition of plagiarism begins with what a writer wants readers to think. By submitting my essay, I show that the argument is mine and that I have acknowledged the contributions of other people. Plagiarism happens when I present someone else's words or thinking as my own. According to Harvard's Guide to Using Sources, paraphrased ideas still need acknowledgment, and changing several words does not constitute an adequate paraphrase ("What Constitutes Plagiarism?"). This standard goes beyond word-for-word copying. Even rewriting every sentence of someone else's argument may constitute plagiarism if I fail to acknowledge its source. The reader needs to understand where the borrowed contribution ends and where mine starts.

In Viswanathan's case, the dispute concerned the language of a commercially published novel, not an uncited research paper. McCafferty's publishing house called the borrowing "literary identity theft," whereas McCafferty's agent reported finding forty-five similar passages (Zhou, "Publisher Rejects"). The term itself is an accusation, not a neutral definition; however, it describes the harm of crediting one writer with another's expression. Without accepting every implication of the term, I can say that the borrowing was serious. The publicly available comparisons allow me to study the accusations instead of relying only on the publisher's indignation.

The clearest comparison is the description of the shopping trip. McCafferty uses the expression "four major department stores and 170 specialty shops later," and Viswanathan writes "Five department stores, and 170 specialty shops later" (qtd. in Zhou, "Student's Novel"). The substitution does little to change the effect. Both passages use an exaggerated tally of shops to describe an exhausting activity. Both place the department stores and specialty shops before the word "later," although that construction is not necessary to describe a tiring shopping trip. The number 170 is especially revealing since a writer could use countless other expressions to make shopping seem exhausting. Thus, keeping that number and adjusting the smaller one seems more like editing someone else's sentence than independent composition.

There is a limit to such reasoning. McCafferty can't claim ownership of the idea that shopping is exhausting, just as a novelist can't claim ownership of teenage insecurity. Zhou gives another example in which buying Diet Cokes at a bakery becomes a joke about dieting. Viswanathan changes Cinnabon to Mrs. Fields ("Student's Novel"). The general contrast between a diet drink and sweets is not unique to any writer. I wouldn't accuse Viswanathan of plagiarism based only on that shared idea. However, the close wording and setup, in combination with other similarities, make the example troubling. This distinction keeps the accusation from becoming too broad: influence, a common theme, and copied expression have to be considered separately.

Viswanathan's explanation makes the discussion difficult. She acknowledged that she had read McCafferty's novels but claimed that she had borrowed some phrases unconsciously (Bhayani and Zhou). I can't determine what Viswanathan was remembering while composing the novel. In his article, Zhou includes psychologist Daniel Schacter's explanation of cryptomnesia ("Publisher Rejects"). Cryptomnesia occurs when someone remembers information but misidentifies its source. It is a possible explanation for accidental borrowing. However, it does not prove that this borrowing was accidental. Still, disregarding that possibility would make my argument easier but not more accurate. The evidence supports a firmer conclusion about the text than about Viswanathan's thoughts.

Even if I accepted Viswanathan's explanation, I would still treat the result as plagiarism. The original author's contribution does not disappear just because the borrowing was unconscious. However, intention plays an important role in choosing the punishment. A student who accidentally paraphrases incorrectly deserves a different response from a student who presents a purchased essay as her own. Both essays, however, need correction. Treating intention as the only criterion would make the original writer depend on a confession. We can identify plagiarism in a text without knowing its author's intentions.

The type of writing also determines the correction that is required. In this essay, quoting the shopping passage is appropriate because I identify the source and analyze the language. The quotation helps the reader evaluate my reasoning. In a novel, borrowed wording presented as the narrator's own language has a different role: it supplies the original prose readers expect the novelist to create. An acknowledgment thanking McCafferty would not guarantee that the passages became acceptable. Viswanathan needed to develop her own narrative wording. Fiction allows influence and allusion; however, these practices can't excuse passing close reproductions off as original writing. The question is whether readers can recognize the borrowing or unknowingly give another writer credit for it.

Little, Brown pulled the book from sale and then announced that it would not issue a revised version of the novel or the second book under Viswanathan's contract (Zhou, "Publisher Pulls"; Crimson Staff). Pulling the novel from sale seems to be the most appropriate measure taken, since continued sales would have continued the misrepresentation. Canceling the second book, however, went beyond the work in which the borrowing had been identified. Nevertheless, I consider this move reasonable because the publisher had agreed to sell original writing, and the first book gave Little, Brown grounds to doubt that Viswanathan had fulfilled that agreement. The company was not obligated to pay for the second book in order to forgive the mistake.

When it comes to academic consequences, a Harvard representative stated at the time that the college's plagiarism policy applied only to work submitted for course credit and that this case did not appear related to coursework (Zhou, "College Looking"). This detail matters, as Viswanathan's student status does not turn her publishing contract into a course assignment. For that reason, I would not automatically impose academic punishment as well. Moreover, public disgrace does not seem to be a useful punishment in itself. Critics should identify the borrowing and describe its negative impact. Future honest work should be evaluated on its merits rather than dismissed because of a single past failure.

It is possible to relate this case to the challenge mentioned in my previous essay: rejecting opening sentences before I give them a chance. I explained that some opening sentences feel too general, while others feel forced, which results in spending too much time writing my paper's introduction. That experience helps me understand the temptation to use a sentence that already works and makes me more sympathetic to Viswanathan's position. However, it does not excuse the borrowing. My desire to find the best sentence immediately makes it especially important to know where that sentence came from. Polishing borrowed wording does not give me ownership of the idea it expresses.

The specific similarities that remained despite the changes justify the plagiarism accusation in this case. The evidence justified withdrawing the book from sale, and the broken trust between the writer and publisher justified canceling the contract. Establishing plagiarism did not require proving that Viswanathan intended to deceive readers, nor did it require judging her entire character. When it comes to my own writing, the harder test is also the more useful one: I should be able to explain my sentence's contribution and acknowledge its sources. Changing four stores to five would not meet that test because the distinctive expression would still belong to someone else.
