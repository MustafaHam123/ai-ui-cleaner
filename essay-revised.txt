The Difference Between Four Stores and Five

Changing four department stores to five is a small revision. It is also a useful place to begin deciding whether a writer has plagiarized. In 2006, The Harvard Crimson published comparisons between Kaavya Viswanathan's novel, How Opal Mehta Got Kissed, Got Wild, and Got a Life, and Megan McCafferty's earlier fiction. One shopping passage changed the number of department stores but kept the oddly specific count of 170 specialty shops (Zhou, "Student's Novel"). I find that detail more persuasive than a general accusation of dishonesty because I can examine what changed and what stayed. The accusation against Viswanathan was justified: her revisions preserved another writer's distinctive expression while presenting it as her own. Withdrawing the novel and canceling her contract were fair responses to that failure, although neither consequence establishes that she deserved permanent exclusion from writing.

My definition of plagiarism begins with what a writer asks the reader to believe. When I submit an essay, my name indicates that I am responsible for its argument and that I have identified contributions belonging to other people. Plagiarism occurs when I let someone else's language or distinctive thinking pass as my own. Harvard's Guide to Using Sources explains that paraphrased ideas still require acknowledgment and that changing a few words does not produce an adequate paraphrase ("What Constitutes Plagiarism?"). That standard reaches beyond exact copying. Someone can rewrite every sentence of another person's argument and still conceal a debt to that person. The reader needs to know where the borrowed contribution ends and the writer's contribution begins.

In Viswanathan's case, the dispute concerned the language of a commercially published novel rather than an uncited research paper. McCafferty's publisher described the borrowing as "literary identity theft," while McCafferty's agent reported finding forty-five similar passages (Zhou, "Publisher Rejects"). The publisher's phrase is an accusation, not a neutral definition, but it identifies the injury at issue: readers would credit Viswanathan with expression that originated elsewhere. I do not need to accept every implication of that phrase to agree that the borrowing was serious. The published comparisons give the accusation a basis that a publisher's anger alone could not provide. They also make it possible to assess the writing without treating every similarity between two novels as theft.

The clearest comparison concerns the shopping trip. McCafferty writes "four major department stores and 170 specialty shops later"; Viswanathan writes "Five department stores, and 170 specialty shops later" (qtd. in Zhou, "Student's Novel"). The substitution does little to change the sentence's effect. Both versions turn shopping into a ridiculous tally, and both deliver the exaggeration through the same sequence of stores, shops, and the word "later." Nobody needs that sequence to describe a tiring afternoon. The number 170 is especially revealing because a writer could choose countless other ways to make shopping sound exhausting. Keeping it while adjusting the smaller number looks less like independent composition than editing a sentence someone else already made work.

There is a limit to that reasoning, however. McCafferty cannot own the idea that shopping is exhausting, any more than a novelist can own teenage insecurity. Zhou also compares passages in which buying Diet Cokes at a bakery becomes a joke about dieting; Viswanathan substitutes Mrs. Fields for McCafferty's Cinnabon ("Student's Novel"). The general contrast between diet soda and sweets could occur to more than one writer. I would hesitate to call that idea alone plagiarism. What makes this example concerning is the close wording and setup, considered alongside the other parallels. That distinction prevents an accusation from becoming too broad: influence, a shared subject, and copied expression need to be evaluated differently.

Viswanathan's explanation makes the judgment more uncomfortable. She acknowledged reading McCafferty's books but said the borrowing was unconscious (Bhayani and Zhou). I cannot establish what she remembered while drafting. In his reporting, Zhou included psychologist Daniel Schacter's explanation of cryptomnesia, a failure to remember the source of remembered information ("Publisher Rejects"). The explanation offers a possible account of accidental borrowing; it does not demonstrate that this particular borrowing was accidental. Still, dismissing the possibility would make the argument easier at the expense of fairness. The evidence supports a conclusion about the text much more securely than it supports a conclusion about everything happening in Viswanathan's mind.

Even if I accepted her explanation, I would still consider the result plagiarism. McCafferty's contribution does not disappear because someone forgets where it came from. Readers still encounter the borrowed expression under a different author's name. Intention matters when deciding how to respond: a student who misunderstands paraphrasing should receive a different judgment from someone who deliberately submits a purchased essay. Both papers, however, need correction. Treating intention as the only requirement would leave the original writer dependent on a confession. We can recognize the misrepresentation in a finished work while remaining uncertain about whether its author planned to deceive anyone.

The kind of writing also affects what correction requires. In this essay, quoting the shopping passage is appropriate because I identify its source and examine its language. The quotation helps the reader check my reasoning. In a novel, borrowed wording presented as the narrator's ordinary speech has a different function: it supplies the writing readers expect the novelist to create. Simply thanking McCafferty would not necessarily make those passages acceptable. Viswanathan needed to develop independent narration. Fiction certainly allows influence and deliberate allusion, but those practices do not justify passing close reproductions off as original prose. The question is whether the borrowing is recognizable as borrowing or quietly receives credit under someone else's name.

Little, Brown first withdrew the novel from stores and later announced that it would publish neither a revised edition nor the second book under Viswanathan's contract (Zhou, "Publisher Pulls"; Crimson Staff). Withdrawing the existing book was the fairest part of the response. Continued sales would have continued the problem, even after the publisher knew about it. Canceling the second book is harder to defend because that manuscript was not the work readers had exposed. Nevertheless, I consider cancellation reasonable. The publisher had agreed to sell original writing, and the first book gave it grounds to doubt that agreement. It was not obligated to finance another book in order to offer forgiveness. The consequence was severe, but it remained connected to the professional relationship that the borrowing had damaged.

The academic consequences require a separate judgment. At the time, a Harvard spokesperson said the college's plagiarism policy applied to work submitted for course credit and that this situation did not appear related to coursework (Zhou, "College Looking"). That detail matters because Viswanathan's identity as a student does not turn her publishing contract into a classroom assignment. I would not automatically add academic punishment to the loss of her book contract. Nor would I treat public disgrace as a useful punishment in itself. Criticism should identify the borrowing and explain the harm. A person who later produces honest work should have that work evaluated fairly, rather than face the assumption that one failure has settled every future question about her ability.

I can connect this case to a difficulty I described in my previous essay: rejecting opening sentences before I give them a chance. I explained that one opening can feel too general and another forced, so I sometimes spend more time beginning a paper than developing it. That frustration helps me understand why a sentence that already works might be tempting, although it does not excuse borrowing it. My preference for a messy first draft also carries a responsibility. When I revise, checking whether a sentence fits my argument is not enough; I need to know where it came from. If I have borrowed someone else's explanation, polishing its vocabulary will not make the thinking mine.

What persuades me about the accusation is ultimately the smallness of the changes. Four stores become five, yet the unusual tally survives. Viswanathan's case shows how plagiarism can remain inside a sentence that a writer has technically revised. The published evidence justified withdrawing the novel, and the broken trust justified ending the contract. It did not require certainty about her intentions or a permanent judgment about her character. For my own writing, the harder standard is also the more useful one: I should be able to account for the contribution a sentence makes, including any part I owe to another writer. That obligation remains even when the sentence sounds right and I badly want to keep it.
