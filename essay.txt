Changing the Words Does Not Make Them Yours

A shopping trip might seem like an unlikely place to find evidence of plagiarism. Yet in Kaavya Viswanathan's 2006 novel, How Opal Mehta Got Kissed, Got Wild, and Got a Life, a detail about visiting 170 specialty shops helped expose borrowing from Megan McCafferty's fiction. The number matters because it makes the resemblance difficult to dismiss as two writers independently describing teenage life (Zhou, "Student's Novel"). This case challenges the assumption that plagiarism disappears when someone changes a few words. The accusation against Viswanathan was justified because her novel appropriated distinctive expression, although uncertainty about her intentions requires a careful distinction between holding her accountable and treating her disgrace as permanent.

I consider plagiarism the presentation of another person's language or distinctive ideas as one's own without appropriate acknowledgment. Harvard's Guide to Using Sources explains that borrowing ideas requires citation even when a writer paraphrases them. It also identifies borrowing sentence structure while changing individual words as an unacceptable paraphrase ("What Constitutes Plagiarism?"). This definition makes sense because authorship includes decisions about how to express a thought. A writer can contribute original material and still plagiarize within the same work. However, the standard needs limits: common phrases and broad subjects cannot belong exclusively to one person. Evidence must establish a meaningful debt to a particular source, rather than a general resemblance between works.

The shopping passage provides that evidence. As reproduced in David Zhou's reporting, McCafferty describes "four major department stores and 170 specialty shops later," while Viswanathan writes "Five department stores, and 170 specialty shops later" (qtd. in Zhou, "Student's Novel"). Changing four stores to five preserves the unusual number of shops and the sentence's comic exaggeration. The joke depends on treating an exhausting shopping trip as an absurd numerical tally. Viswanathan retains that mechanism along with much of its wording. Someone describing shopping independently could mention crowded stores, aching feet, or indecision. Repeating this particular tally creates a stronger connection to McCafferty's expression than simply using the same setting would.

Another comparison shows why evaluating isolated matching words can miss the problem. Zhou reports that McCafferty describes buying Diet Cokes at Cinnabon as a self-punishing dietary choice; Viswanathan uses a closely related description but substitutes Mrs. Fields ("Student's Novel"). The business name changes, while the contrast between a diet drink and a place associated with sweets remains. That contrast supplies the humor. These examples persuade me because the similarities involve choices that neither the subject nor ordinary grammar requires. A teenage character does not have to count 170 shops or make this particular observation about soda. The accusation rests on distinctive expression carried into another book, not ownership of teenage experiences.

Still, similarity does not automatically reveal intention. Viswanathan acknowledged reading McCafferty's novels and maintained that the borrowing happened unconsciously (Bhayani and Zhou). Her explanation deserves consideration rather than immediate ridicule. In Zhou's reporting on the response, Harvard psychologist Daniel Schacter explained cryptomnesia as remembering information while misidentifying its source ("Publisher Rejects"). This gives an accidental explanation some plausibility; it does not establish that it explains Viswanathan's conduct. The distinction matters. A reader can recognize unattributed borrowing without claiming access to the author's thoughts. I find the published parallels convincing evidence of plagiarism, but I cannot prove from those passages alone whether she deliberately copied them or sincerely mistook remembered language for her own.

Accepting that uncertainty does not excuse the published result. A reader encounters the words on the page, not the writer's private memory. If borrowed expression appears as original narration, the earlier writer loses acknowledgment regardless of the explanation. Intention should therefore influence the response to plagiarism without determining whether borrowing occurred. An accidental mistake might justify instruction and revision where deliberate deception warrants a stronger penalty. Either way, the writer has a responsibility to address the mistake. Otherwise, an author could claim unconscious influence whenever a reader identified copying, leaving the original writer with little protection. Sympathy for someone who makes a serious mistake should not require ignoring whose work appears in the finished text.

The differences between fiction and academic essays also help define the offense. An essay can quote McCafferty, cite her, and analyze her language because readers understand that the quotation supplies evidence. A novel's narration generally presents its sentences as the novelist's own composition. Adding an acknowledgment would not necessarily make extensive borrowed narration acceptable. In my view, Viswanathan needed to create independent expression rather than merely attach credit to passages that remained close to another book. Originality does not require inventing a subject no one has considered before. It requires doing the expressive work that the publication asks readers to attribute to its author.

Little, Brown initially withdrew the novel from stores; it later announced that it would publish neither a revised edition nor Viswanathan's second contracted book (Zhou, "Publisher Pulls"; Crimson Staff). Withdrawing the existing novel was fair. Continuing to sell it would have continued the misrepresentation and asked McCafferty to tolerate it. Canceling the second book is harder to assess because it penalized future work as well as the existing publication. Even so, I consider the decision defensible: a publisher depends on an author's ability to deliver original writing. The discovery gave Little, Brown a reason to question that trust. Losing this contract was a consequence connected to the failure, rather than an unrelated punishment imposed simply to embarrass her.

That judgment should not become a demand for permanent exclusion. A canceled contract establishes a boundary around a publisher's investment; it does not establish that a young writer can never learn to work responsibly. I would distinguish that commercial decision from a university's obligation to educate its students. The same evidence can justify withdrawing a book while leaving room for instruction, supervised revision, and later work judged on its merits. Fairness requires attention to the harm and to the purpose of each consequence. Public criticism can explain the copying and defend McCafferty's authorship. Personal humiliation does neither, and it should not become a substitute for evaluating the work.

This case also changes how I understand the writing process I described in my previous essay. I explained that I often reject opening sentences too early and prefer building an argument after producing a messy draft. That approach helps me begin, but a rough draft still needs boundaries between my thinking and borrowed material. A polished sentence can be tempting precisely because it solves the problem I am struggling with. During revision, I need to ask where that sentence came from, not only whether it sounds effective. The Viswanathan case shows why changing a few details cannot replace the work of developing an explanation that I can honestly call my own.

Viswanathan's case supports a firm judgment without requiring an absolute judgment about her character. The specific parallels justify the plagiarism accusation, and withdrawing the novel addressed a continuing harm. Canceling the contract was defensible, although those consequences should leave room for future improvement. For my writing, the lesson is practical: originality must survive revision. A sentence is not mine simply because it fits my paragraph or because I have changed its vocabulary. I must be able to explain the thinking behind it and acknowledge whatever another writer contributed.
